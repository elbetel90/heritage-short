# Design

## Audience

The film is for people about 16 to 35 who already feel the absurd in the sense of Camus's *The Stranger* and *The Myth of Sisyphus*. They ask what a life is for, and the world returns no answer. That silence is the experience the film is about.

They watch on a phone. The primary frame is vertical, 9:16. The 16:9 export is the same film for a screen that is not a phone.

## Intent

A viewer should leave holding two things at once. The absurd is real, and it is not removed by looking away. What remains is a small light inside this life: accept the life they actually have, stay lucid, keep going, and care for a person they love.

Diversion is any habit that turns the mind off the silence without answering it. Religion used as a leap, a total scientific or political explanation, substance use, status, a career that is supposed to make a person legitimate, a romance treated as the missing meaning, scrolling and noise, shopping and self-improvement, "everything happens for a reason," waiting for a worthier life, and cynicism that refuses to care are all the same move. This film uses one of them: late-night self-improvement podcasts, habit streaks, and checklists. It does not treat that habit as an answer.

The ending is not a solved crisis. The silence is still there. The character knows the morning will not thank him, and he does the small human thing anyway. That act is the light. Hope in this film is tea left for someone who is asleep. It is not a rescue from outside this life.

## Story

Tarek counts his nights so they will finally mean something. He writes down his sister's shift and still will not say why it matters. The podcast ends, and he leaves tea outside her door.

Tarek is fictional, in his early twenties, and shares a small apartment with his younger sister, Nadia. She is fictional too. She never appears and she never thanks him. The door stays closed. Neither face is a real person's likeness. The consent record is in `docs/ETHICS.md`.

Narration is English, in short clauses, spoken by the Schedar voice: dry, low, and unsentimental. The word "absurd" is never spoken. The burned-in subtitles are the same English lines. Two shots in the middle are room tone only, so the silence can be heard. Subjects sit in the center of the 9:16 frame so the 16:9 crop still holds them. The film does not end on a smile, a sunrise that redeems him, or Nadia opening the door.

## User tests

A user test is a showing, not a software check. Three people who are like the audience watch a cut. Their words are written here, and the storyboard changes because of what they said. The radio cut has been played to two people. One more person still has to hear it. The near-final showing has not happened yet.

### Radio cut

The cut is a black screen, Tarek's English narration, and a quiet placeholder score. It is played before any keyframe, to at least three people about 16 to 35 who know this kind of restlessness. The questions are whether the silence lands when the episode ends and the fridge keeps going, and whether the mug left for someone who does not open the door feels like a small hope or like a puzzle.

Played on 25 November 2026 to two people. They said the silence lands when the episode ends. The fridge keeping going is not audible. The mug outside the door is not visible, so it is hard to judge whether that gesture feels like a small hope.

One more person, about 16 to 35, still has to hear this cut. The spoken lines stay as they are. The fridge question waits for a later mix. The mug question waits for the near-final cut.

### Maker's listen, 25 November 2026

Heard on the 88-second stereo cut.

The first listen did not stay one person. The opening was thin and started too suddenly. At about 20 seconds the voice dropped and got heavier. At about 28 seconds there was a heavy breath. Schedar stays the voice. Spoken again on 25 November 2026 on one model: a short pause before the first word, the same steady pace through the opening, and "He was still talking" with no gasp.
